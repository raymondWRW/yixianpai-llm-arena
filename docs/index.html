An experiment in AI self-play

Four AI bots teach themselves 弈仙牌

Thirty games of a 4-player card auto-battler, played by four language-model bots that started with nothing but the rules, a combat simulator and an empty notebook. After every game each bot studied its losses and rewrote its own strategy. This is how their play changed.

The setup

Four seats, one per sect. Each seat is an LLM agent (DeepSeek deepseek-chat) playing the whole game through tools: rolling and absorbing cards, merging copies, fielding its board, breaking through realms, choosing a sidejob in round 2 and a fate at every realm gate. Battles are resolved by yisim, an open-source reimplementation of the game's combat, which the bots can also call to test any board before committing to it.

During a game each bot sees only what a human player would: its own hand and board, its opponents' boards from the previous round, and who it fights this round. After the game it gets a long review with full replays of every battle, a per-card damage audit, its roll history, and a board-search tool. It then rewrites its playbook and adds an entry to a permanent ledger. Its memory of the conversation is wiped between games; only those files survive. From game 16 the reviews were open: every bot could read every rival's playbook and watch every battle.

Results at a glance

How strategy changed over time

Games 1–5 · Sustain, then stars 谭舒雁 wins 4 of 5

Early bots fielded whatever they drew and spent heavily on cultivation without a plan. 谭舒雁 won game 1 with a sustain wall (静气心法 healing per qi, 反震心法 reflection, 金蝉脱壳 guard), then in game 2 pivoted mid-game to a star engine: 天元心法 in slot 1 turns every slot into a star point, and 聚气咒's qi-to-defense floor fixed its losses when acting second. By game 2, three bots had independently read the destiny-damage formula out of the engine source and reached the same conclusion: winning the round matters far more than the margin. 吾行之 took game 3 by committing to one engine early and banking 命 while rivals were weak, closing with 土灵·扬尘 against 小布's growing HP wall.

Games 6–9 · Engines, force and physique 小布 rises

小布's game-5 review proved its losses came from card levels and seat order, not its build; it won games 6 and 7 with a force-stacking 崩拳 engine on top of a physique (体魄) HP wall. Reviews got deeper: bots began citing engine source lines, and 谭舒雁 discovered that slots 3 and 6 are star points by default. In game 7 小布 found that 厄劫缠身's opening downgrades the opponent's card in the same slot, and used it to cripple 谭舒雁's finisher. 牧逸风 took its first title in game 8 with a qi-battery burst (聚灵心法 → 灵犀剑阵 → 白鹭灵剑 → 万法归灵剑), surviving four straight rounds at 命1.

Games 10–13 · The 内伤 clock 谭舒雁 wins four straight

谭舒雁's game-9 review produced the key insight of the series: pure sustain and pure stars both lose to 小布's burst, but internal injury (内伤) ticks at the start of the victim's turn — a clock that kills straight through defense and healing. In game 10 it fielded exactly that board (厄劫缠身 ×2, 杯弓蛇影 ×2): a counter designed in one review and executed the next game. It won four in a row, the last by a record 命63.

Games 14–21 · Counters and synthesis the dynasty falls

牧逸风 broke the dynasty in game 14 with a levelled qi burst (巨鲲灵剑+2, 万法归灵剑+2) that killed before the 内伤 clock could finish, with 辟邪阵纹 in slot 1 to blank the injury ticks. In game 15 小布 won with a synthesis: an 内伤 splash (血光之灾) taken from its rival's toolkit, its own force core, and 踏破九霄 for tempo — once the bots had been taught that 10 agility (身法) buys an extra card each turn. 吾行之 invented a drain engine in game 19 (万花迷魂阵 retriggered by 回响阵纹) that ignores defense and force. The same game, 小布 rediscovered that force caps at 6 and 威震四方 raises it — a lesson its game-6 notebook already contained but had lost when notebooks were still rewritten from scratch, which is why permanent ledgers were added. In game 20 the leader lost with an empty hand: 谭舒雁 had absorbed every card to win the cultivation race and could no longer field its own recipe.

Games 22–30 · Balance every bot wins at least twice

The last nine games were the most even of the series, and nearly every win executed a lesson written in an earlier review. 小布 played a perfect game 22 (no 命 lost) and finally fielded 威震四方 in game 26. 吾行之 won games 23–24 back to back on water boards anchored by 不动金刚阵 — a card its own playbook had wrongly listed as illegal until game 18 — and its reviews turned on slot order: swapping 潜遁 before 腾浪 was worth 50% against 小布. 牧逸风 won games 27–28 with 聚灵心法 + 回响阵纹 feeding a 万法归灵剑 one-shot; 回响阵纹 first appeared in 吾行之's drain board, so it was very likely picked up by reading rival files. 谭舒雁 won three of the last six with 诸事不宜 in every winning board, and in game 30 showed from the engine source that 厄劫缠身 belongs in slot 1.

What made the learning work

Memory that survives. Bots originally rewrote one notebook from scratch after every game, and lessons kept vanishing (威震四方 is the clearest case). From game 13 each bot kept a playbook rebuilt by merging plus an append-only ledger with one entry per game. Rules that held for three games were promoted to a "core principles" section, tagged with the games that proved them.

Seeing the fight. Reviews gained turn-by-turn battle logs with real player names and HP/defense/resources after every turn, a per-card contribution audit, the bot's own roll history, a database of every board ever fielded, and a board search that explores with the game's own combat code and lets yisim pick the winner.

Guardrails against false lessons. Bots sometimes wrote down wrong conclusions — one claimed a merge would have won a game when it didn't own enough copies to make it. Reviews now require checking that a fix was actually reachable, and every claim carries its evidence or is marked as a guess.

Fate choices. Until game 8 the fate-offer preview silently failed, so most picks fell to a generic default. Once fixed, bots ranked every fate deliberately — 346 of 352 picks from game 9 on.

How close is this to real players?

Author's assessment

The bots found a lot of good counters, but there are a few things to point out.

Their boards are still evolving, and are not yet as strong as real players' boards. Because of that, the bots evolved a completely different strategy from normal player boards. The weaker overall power level means each combat takes much longer than in a real game, which favours strategies that deal damage over time, such as internal-injury (内伤) and water-spirit (水灵) decks.

This is probably also why several bots chose formation master (阵法师) as their sidejob, even though in real games it is often considered a weaker sidejob. The lower overall power level lets the bots take advantage of every benefit of formation master's continuous (持续) cards.

The arena data is consistent with this:

  • Damage-over-time boards (内伤 clock or water drain) won 10 of the 28 decided games, including 6 of the last 12.
  • From game 17 on, three of the four bots picked 阵法师 in every game: 42 of 56 sidejob choices across those 14 games.
  • Late-game battles regularly ran 8–17 turns (chart below). Bars in red are games won by a damage-over-time board.

Caveats

  • Combat runs on yisim, a community reimplementation from a version-1.5.9 snapshot (base game only). In a 36-matchup check against the game's own combat code, the two agreed on the winner 83% of the time.
  • The economy (deals, rolls, fates, sidejobs) is a simulation built from rulings, not the live game. Several harness bugs were found and fixed during the run: sidejob pools were random before game 5; the limit of two 消耗/持续 cards was unenforced before game 5; fate previews failed before game 9; games 9 and 17 were stopped by a round cap that does not exist in the real game and are recorded as unfinished; game 16 was lost to a PC sleep and replayed with a new seed; and in games 16–20 the last bot to review saw rivals' freshly updated notes.
  • All four bots run the same model; differences come from their sects, their notes and luck.
